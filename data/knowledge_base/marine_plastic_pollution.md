# Marine Plastic Pollution and Microplastic Dynamics

**Document ID:** KB-DOC-001  
**Category:** Marine Pollution Information  
**Source:** Marine Environmental Research Review & Coastal Litter Management Guidelines  

## 1. Overview of Marine Plastics
Plastics constitute between 60% and 80% of total marine debris globally. Common marine plastics comprise low-density polyethylene (LDPE), high-density polyethylene (HDPE), polypropylene (PP), and polyethylene terephthalate (PET). Due to their high buoyancy and polymer stability, plastics persist in oceanic currents and coastal tidal zones for decades to centuries.

## 2. Environmental and Ecological Significance
- **Macro-debris Ingestion:** Marine turtles, seabirds (such as shearwaters and albatrosses), and marine mammals frequently mistake floating plastic bags, bottle caps, and packaging films for natural prey such as jellyfish or squid. Ingestion causes physical blockage of the digestive tract, false satiation, starvation, and internal ulceration.
- **Photodegradation and Microplastics:** Exposure to ultraviolet (UV) solar radiation and mechanical wave action causes brittle macroplastics to fracture into secondary microplastics (particles smaller than 5 millimeters).
- **Chemical Adsorption:** Microplastics possess a hydrophobic surface that adsorbs persistent organic pollutants (POPs), including polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) and organochlorine pesticides, from seawater. Ingested microplastics can transfer these toxins into marine trophic webs.

## 3. Recommended Field Response and Handling
- **Tidal Zone Retrieval:** Collect plastic debris during low tide before high-water tidal cycles refloat and transport fragments offshore into pelagic currents.
- **Segregation:** Sort recovered plastics into clean rigid polymers (PET, HDPE bottles) suitable for mechanical recycling, and contaminated or degraded fragments destined for municipal non-hazardous waste streams.
- **Protective Equipment:** Field personnel should utilize puncture-resistant puncture-proof gloves, closed-toe coastal boots, and UV protection when handling degraded brittle plastics that may produce sharp micro-splinters.

## 4. Monitoring Considerations
- Note the level of biofouling (algae and barnacle encrustation), which indicates the duration of time the plastic debris has spent adrift at sea.
- Record polymer brand, manufacturing country markings (if legible), and product type to assist in identifying point-source or commercial origin in coastal debris surveys.
